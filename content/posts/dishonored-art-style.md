## Short answer

The two developer interviews reviewed here do not establish a single official name for Dishonored's visual style. Arkane's developers described a mix of 19th-century London and Edinburgh, maritime costume, Renaissance architecture, European folklore and 1940s book illustration, deliberately stylized rather than photorealistic. The city is Dunwall; the visual directors were Viktor Antonov and Sebastien Mitton. Both sources are interviews from 2012, around the game's release, not design documents from the start of production.

## What it looks like

A dense industrial city of brick, soot, whale-oil lamps, plague barriers, rats and heavy atmospheric light, with characters whose proportions and costumes are exaggerated rather than realistic. Antonov said the team wanted "slightly exaggerated characters, unusual costumes like the ones you can see in book illustrations of pirate adventures." Mitton's research produced "costumes echoing England's maritime fleet," while the architecture drew on "the beginning of the Elizabethan and Jacobean Renaissance."

The look is not a historical reconstruction. Antonov called Dunwall "a parallel dimension, even if it's inspired by 19th century London." That phrase explains why labels like Victorian or steampunk are useful shorthand but incomplete.

## What the developers said

The starting point was not London. In the [VICE interview](https://www.vice.com/en/article/behind-the-scenes-of-idishonoredi-an-interview-with-the-creators-of-the-epic-dystopian-game/), Mitton explained: "At that time, Dishonored was supposed to take place in Japan during medieval times, but we told them it was hardly conceivable—as Europeans, we can easily fall into cheap stereotypes. After several discussions, we told them that we wanted the game to be based in London, which was our starting point." A separate [2012 preview report](https://www.shacknews.com/article/73538/the-unmistakably-english-art-of-dishonored) adds Antonov's reason for London: "London was chosen as an inspiration because it is perceived as mysterious to Americans and other Europeans."

Antonov described the aim: "We created something that can't be identified as the past or the future, but as a parallel dimension." Mitton gave the research trail — an early plague-era idea felt "way too far back," 1666 costumes were "absolutely ridiculous," and he settled on maritime costume and early Renaissance architecture. He also described visits to libraries and museums, and a study trip to Edinburgh prompted partly by videos of cyclist Danny MacAskill, where the low light helped shape the atmosphere.

On sources outside games, Antonov was explicit: "by dint of copying, we end up with flat and uninteresting works. In order to avoid this, we go straight to the source." He named 1940s illustrators Dean Cornwell and J. C. Leyendecker, and traditional tales such as Hoffmann's *The Nutcracker and the Mouse King*. Mitton said he did not take visual inspiration from other games.

Neither interview presents a studio-coined name for the look. This only describes the two sources reviewed, not every statement Arkane may have made elsewhere.

## How the visual direction was developed

The interviews describe a design process rather than a single rendering trick. Antonov said a video game's depth comes from backstory: "Creation goes through design, drawings, and the conception of things that will be visible in the game."

Character design was tied to how players read the protagonist. "They are generally quite stylized and simplified, because we wanted the player to identify with Corvo," Antonov said. "Our endeavor was to give free reign to the players' imaginations, and that requires a limited amount of information for them to project themselves." This is a functional reason for the look, not just taste.

Antonov's strongest statement rejects realism outright: "photorealism in art is not interesting"; "Photorealism is a false quest, and once you achieve it, it doesn't tell you anything." He said he tried to make games "as abstract and stylized as I can to mobilize media like special effects and lights to convey things I can't do in everyday life." Lighting and atmosphere were therefore part of the visual method, not decoration.

The developers also used real cities as source material. Antonov: "real cities are like true characters because each has a story and a strong atmosphere." Mitton described how Edinburgh's habit of keeping lights on during the day "was a great vector for our universe."

## Common confusions

**Is it Victorian?** That is shorthand, not a full description. Antonov described a parallel dimension inspired by 19th-century London; Mitton cited maritime costume, Elizabethan and Jacobean architecture, and Edinburgh's lighting. A single period label loses the mixture.

**Is it steampunk?** The 2012 preview report uses the word, but the developers' own explanations point to specific sources — maritime England, illustration, plague imagery, real cities — rather than to the genre label. Treat steampunk as an outside categorization.

**Did one person design the look?** The sources identify Antonov as visual design director and Mitton as art director, and both describe distinct contributions. The VICE interview also names other team members without quoting them. The evidence supports two lead roles, not sole authorship by one famous artist.
