## Short answer

The Wind Waker uses a toon-shaded, cartoon-inspired art direction. Nintendo's developers described the look as cel-shaded and said it enabled more expressive character animation and clearer puzzle information. It replaced a realistic direction shown at Space World 2000; the toon look was revealed in 2001. The explanations below come from an interview given years later, around the 2013 HD release.

## What it looks like

Flat areas of colour with dark outlines, a round-headed Link with large eyes, and watercolour-like sea and sky. Characters move with exaggerated, elastic gestures. In the official interview, Takizawa recalled that seeing the new Link drawing made him think: "With a character like that, we can give him actions that will look and feel good no matter how he moves!" The style covers characters and the world together, but the developers' clearest justifications concern how characters move and how objects read.

## What the developers said

Start with a common date error. Iwata states that the game "was released in 2002" and that "the year before, in 2001" Nintendo revealed what the upcoming Zelda would look like. He then adds that "the year before, in 2000 when we announced the Nintendo GameCube," the company showed "a demo video of an evolved form of Link from The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time." So the realistic tech demo belongs to **2000**, and the toon-shaded reveal to **2001**. A 1999 Space World demo is a different event and is not the one these developers are describing.

Aonuma said the toon look "wasn't what I had at first proposed." Takizawa explained that the team, including Haruhana, "had been trying to figure out which graphical direction to take" and "wondered whether continuing the path taken by Ocarina of Time, and evolving upon it by giving it more detail was really the right path." Aonuma was direct about the prototype: "it was incredibly normal and didn't exceed expectations."

The turn came from a drawing. Takizawa: "Then one day, out of the blue, Haruhana-san hit us with that new Link." Nintendo's own page identifies Yoshiki Haruhana as design manager alongside Satoru Takizawa. He sketched a Moblin in response, and Aonuma said "it suddenly got interesting, with ideas coming out at an incredible speed." Iwata's summary: "Haruhana-san's sketch was a detonator for the core staff that set off a bunch of ideas."

The terms used in this one interview are "toon-shaded" and "cel-shaded." That is not evidence that no other name was ever used by Nintendo; it is only what appears in the source reviewed here.

## How the look was developed

The developers frame the choice as both creative and functional. Takizawa said the team worried that staying on the Ocarina path would limit new ideas, because "while a game is more than its visuals, it was going to be made mostly by the same people, and the ideas we had within the same team has its limits."

The functional argument was readability. Takizawa: "Another benefit of those visuals was how we could represent the mechanisms and objects for puzzles in a more easy-to-understand way. When the visuals are photorealistic, it had the adverse effect of making information difficult to represent game-wise." Asked by Iwata about the problem, he clarified: "You can't tell what moves and what you should touch. But if you change the visual style of parts that move, it'll stand out like a sore thumb."

Aonuma tied the style to combat and committed to it early: "we felt we achieved a new sense of combat against new enemies thanks to the stylized actions performed by cel-shaded characters. We decided rather early on that we definitely wanted to go with that direction." The through-line is that the look arrived as a character sketch, enabled expressive animation, made puzzle objects legible, and then shaped how enemies and fights were designed.

## Common confusions

**Was the realistic demo the 1999 one?** Not in this interview. Iwata explicitly places the realistic Link demo at Space World 2000 and the toon-shaded reveal at Space World 2001. Converting one into the other changes the timeline the developers describe.

**Was the style invented to imitate cartoons?** The interview does not present it as an exercise in nostalgia. The stated reasons were a wish to move beyond the Ocarina visual path, the creative spark of Haruhana's Link drawing, and the readability of puzzle objects.

**Did cel shading alone create the look?** The source discusses character design, animation, enemy design and puzzle readability together. Treating the shader as the whole decision leaves out most of what the developers said.
