## A game arrived before its name

In 1994, Koei released *Angelique* for the Super Famicom. Players took the role of a young woman competing to become queen of the universe, aided by nine male guardians whose affection they could raise. It is generally described as the first otome game. But at the time, nobody used that word.

![The original Angelique on Super Famicom: a full-screen dialogue portrait over a palace scene](/img/editorial/angelique-1994-gameplay.webp)
*The 1994 original: a text box, a portrait and a palace. The format looks close to a visual novel, but its 1994 marketing called it a "romance simulation game," not an otome game — and not a visual novel either.*

According to the [Wikipedia entry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Otome_game), early promotional materials called *Angelique* a "Neo Romance game," and the term "otome game" "did not emerge until late 2001 to around 2002." [VICE's history of Ruby Party](https://www.vice.com/en/article/all-woman-team-otome-angelique-ruby-party/) describes it as "marketed towards young women as a 'romantic SLG' (romance simulation game)." Available accounts trace the word to reader letters, surveys and dōjinshi in magazines such as *Dengeki G's* and *B's-LOG*, where female players used *otome* — "maiden" — to describe themselves. Publishers then adopted the audience's word as the genre's name.

That account should be held lightly. The primary sources behind it — early print issues and Koei's original promotional material — were not directly available for this article, so the precise route from readership to genre label is best described as the current published explanation rather than a fully traced history. What is well supported is the sequence: the game came first, and the name may have taken roughly seven years to arrive.

That is a more interesting origin story than a definition. A genre's name was not handed down by developers. It grew out of the people playing.

## Why Ruby Party made it

The team behind *Angelique* was Koei's Ruby Party, [founded in 1990](https://www.vice.com/en/article/all-woman-team-otome-angelique-ruby-party/) at the suggestion of Keiko Erikawa, who had co-founded Koei in 1978. VICE reports that after *Nobunaga's Ambition* in 1983, Koei had one woman on its development team, and that Erikawa "decided that there needed to be more opportunities for women in game development, and more games aimed at women."

The date line here needs care. Ruby Party itself was founded in 1990 and shipped *Angelique* in 1994 — four years, not ten. The "over 10 years" in the same VICE article refers to a longer clock: the time between Koei's post-1983 effort to recruit and train women developers and the game's 1994 release. Few women studied programming at the time, so many hires came from humanities backgrounds. Erikawa's recollection, via a *Famitsu* interview: "It took a long time to get our female staff used to making games. None of the women we hired had any experience making games." And on the market: "There were some who said, 'The market is small. If you make games for women, they won't sell.' But I thought, 'The market is there.' I'm glad I stuck to my conviction."

That friction is the part worth remembering. This was not a marketing category invented for a proven audience. A team had to be built first, and the audience had to be argued for.

## The heroine grew up with the players

One detail captures how the genre has changed better than any feature list. The original heroine was 17, because the core audience was teenage girls; a 2021 4Gamer interview with producer Akiko Itō and Mei Erikawa, [summarised in English by VGDensetsu](https://vgdensetsu.net/angelique/), explains that Mei herself played the original as a test player and that the character was "basically a projection of herself."

When the series returned with a new entry in May 2021, the protagonist was 25 and had been working as an employee for three years. The reason given was social change: women no longer had to stop working when they married or had children. The team also had to avoid losing players still in high school, who did not share that experience.

The English here is VGDensetsu's summary of a Japanese interview, not the interviewees' own words in English. But the substance is clear: the same series moved its female lead from 17 to 25 because the women playing her had grown up. "Otome game" is not a fixed product template. It is a category that has shifted with the age and life stage of its audience.

## What the label means today

With that history in place, a working definition is possible. An otome game is a story game built around a female protagonist's perspective, in which romance with one or more usually male leads is a main goal alongside the plot. The word *otome* (乙女) means "maiden" or "young woman," so the name points at the intended audience rather than at a mechanic.

It helps to stop treating genre labels as a family tree. Three labels answer three different questions:

| Term | Question it answers |
| :--- | :--- |
| Otome game | Who is it for, and whose romance does it tell? |
| Visual novel | How does it present its story? |
| Dating sim | What simulation mechanics develop the relationship? |

Those dimensions can combine in different ways. An otome game can be told as a visual novel, and many modern console titles are. It can also use stats, schedules and dating mechanics — the original *Angelique* raised affection levels while the player competed to populate a continent. A visual novel may contain no romance at all. A dating sim need not be aimed at women or framed through a female lead.

This is why claims such as "all otome games are visual novels" should be treated as one writer's useful shorthand, not a law. [VNPaths](https://vnpaths.com/?p=6928/) frames otome games within the visual novel format and notes that "most modern otome games — particularly console releases on Nintendo Switch and PlayStation — are visual novels with narrative choices rather than simulation games with stat management." That is a fair observation about current releases. It is not a formal taxonomy agreed by every publisher.

## Where it overlaps with dating sims

The confusion has a genuine source: the first game was marketed as a romance simulation. But the labels are doing different jobs. Our [Visual Novel vs Dating Sim](/posts/visual-novel-vs-dating-sim.html/) guide draws the mechanic boundary through calendars, stats, scheduled dates and affection meters.

Wikipedia's otome entry describes "simulation otome games" where "there is also other gameplay which affects the plot, either by playing minigames or by raising stats," alongside a "pure dating aspect" of being asked out and replying to a love interest. VICE's account of *Angelique* gives the concrete version: helping a guardian raised their affection and improved the protagonist's standing in the population contest.

Modern releases often look different. The screenshot below is from a current otome title: the same dialogue box and character art are doing the work, while the interface is closer to a narrative visual novel than a schedule screen.

![A modern otome game dialogue: character portrait, name tag and choice text](/img/editorial/otome-modern-dialogue.webp)
*A modern otome game: the romance is still the organising goal, but the visible interface is text, portrait and choices — the visual novel form rather than a management screen.*

So the practical test is not "does it have romance?" Both labels do. It is "is the romance built as a simulation?" If play mainly revolves around managing time, stats and dates, dating sim is the closer mechanic. If it revolves around reading routes and choosing dialogue that steers a relationship, otome is the closer genre label — and visual novel describes the form underneath.

| If the main activity is… | The useful label is… |
| :--- | :--- |
| Reading text, advancing scenes, choosing narrative branches | Visual novel |
| Managing time, stats or affection to build a relationship | Dating sim |
| Following a female lead's romance across several male leads | Otome game |
| All of the above | All three, in different dimensions |
