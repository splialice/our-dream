"Fake OS" is the label a group of more than 150 developers and publishers agreed to use in 2026 for games that simulate a device or an operating system as a core mechanic. It is a convention, not a ruling. The group's own site lists four other names and says they are all equally valid, and as of 12 November 2026 Fake OS could not be verified as a Valve-established Steam tag.

## The definition, from the people asking for the name

InterfaceX, the developer-led group behind the campaign, defines the term in a footnote on [its own site](https://interfacex.net/):

> "For us, this means a major part of the game simulates an actual device and/or operating system (desktop, phone, browser, console, etc.) as a major gameplay mechanic."

The same footnote names the alternatives — "Interface Sims, Interface Dramas, Desktop/Phone Sims, and Found Device games" — and ends with the most useful sentence in the document:

> "We agreed to use the Fake OS tag on Steam to simplify things, but all other names are equally valid."

"We agreed" is the answer to the question in the title. No platform, publication or standards body handed down a name; a group picked one so that a scattered set of games could be found in one place. The words "as a major gameplay mechanic" do the rest of the work. A game does not qualify simply because it contains a fictional phone or computer. The simulated device must play a major role in how the game works — it needs to be a core gameplay mechanic, not just a decorative interface.

*The Roottrees Are Dead* is a useful example: its fictional computer provides the environment for the game's central research and deduction mechanics. Its [search and notebook system](/posts/why-roottrees-search-was-simple.html/) is documented as development history, not as a genre classification. Another is [*Hypnospace Outlaw*](/posts/why-hypnospace-outlaw-needed-zones.html/): finding pages inside its fictional web is the task itself, so the simulated browser had to be made navigable before the game could be played.

## Is Fake OS an official Steam tag?

No official tag could be verified as of 12 November 2026. Three dated checks:

| Date | Source | What it actually says |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 14 April 2026 | [Game Developer, unedited Games Press release](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/press-release/150-game-devs-promote-fake-os-steam-tag-for-interfacex26-steam-sale-showcase) | 150+ developers came together "with the simple goal to make 'Fake OS' an official Steam tag" — a goal, not a result |
| April 2026 | [InterfaceX Tag Helper](https://interfacex.net/tag) | "We hope that enough consistent bottom-up tagging makes it easier for Valve to recognize Fake OS as an official tag" |
| 12 November 2026 | Steam tag URLs, checked directly | `/tag/en/Dwarf/` returns a tag page titled "Dwarves"; `/tag/en/Fake%20OS/` falls back to the store home page |

The tag helper is the clearest evidence. It tells players to click the plus button next to a game's tags, paste `Fake OS` and wait for a checkmark; Steam's own form calls this "adding a new user-defined tag". A user-defined tag is what players supply. An official tag is what Valve adds. The whole campaign exists to move one word from the first bucket to the second.

The site now opens with "InterfaceX26 has wrapped!", yet its headline still reads "150+ devs and you are making Fake OS an official Steam tag" and the tag helper remains the first item on its timeline. The sale ended on 4 May; the request did not.

## Why a tag was worth organising for

Organiser Alexander Zacherl, in the 14 April press release:

> "Even as someone designing a Fake OS game for more than a year, it took me months to put together this list of games, with lots of support from the community. We still find new ones every week! We should really make it easier for the developers following after us. Let's get this Steam tag off the ground together."

The same release says the genre "has never had a clear name" and describes the campaign as "following in the footsteps of fellow devs who rallied for Valve to create a Dwarf Steam tag in 2024". That precedent explains the choice of target. A tag makes a genre browsable in the place where people actually buy games; a critic's coinage or an encyclopedia entry does not put a game in front of a shopper. The campaign ran a tag helper from 14 April, a sale of nearly 100 games from 27 April to 4 May, and a live showcase on 2 May.

## Two different kinds of "official"

A studio can name its own work, and a platform can add a category. Gearbox did the first in 2009 when it adopted [Concept Art Style](/posts/borderlands-art-style.html/) while telling everyone else they could use whatever term they liked. InterfaceX is asking Valve for the second. Neither kind of naming settles what the games are; both settle how they can be found.
