The Borderlands shown to the public in 2007 and the Borderlands that shipped in 2009 barely look like the same game. One was a brown, gritty science-fiction shooter. The other had ink outlines, hand-painted textures and characters with impossible proportions.

The usual shorthand — that Gearbox simply "added cel shading" — misses two distinctions. The studio explicitly rejected that label while the game was still in development, and the visual overhaul was not a post-processing filter applied to an otherwise finished game. It involved a broader production pipeline: hand-drawn textures, software-generated graphic-novel outlines, carefully shaped shadows and redesigned character proportions. It was also a decision, taken with the project roughly three-quarters finished, to throw out a large part of a game people had been building for years. Gearbox documented the whole thing at GDC in 2010, in a talk whose title doubles as a warning: `Borderlands & The 11th Hour Art Style Change (Don't Try This at Home)`.

This page is about how the change happened. The separate question of what the finished look should be called is answered in [What Is the Borderlands Art Style Called?](/posts/borderlands-art-style.html/) and [Is Borderlands Cel Shaded?](/posts/is-borderlands-cel-shaded.html/).

## Six directions, and the safe one

Work began in April 2005 under a concept the team summarised as "Halo meets Diablo" — intense first-person action crossed with, as creative director Mikey Neumann put it at GDC, "a game that had loot coming out of every orifice." Before anything was built, the team assembled style sheets with concept words pinned to examples from other media. [GameSpot's report of the 2010 session](https://www.gamespot.com/articles/behind-borderlands-11th-hour-style-change/1100-6253257/) records the direction that won: `Serious`, `Gritty`, `Fantastic`, `Retro`, `Realistic`, `Mechanical`.

Other candidates were wilder. The same report lists a high sci-fi look, a macho look, and an anime-inflected one that Neumann joked someone should go make so he could play it. A 2025 [Game Informer interview](https://gameinformer.com/2025/09/01/the-behind-the-scenes-story-of-the-art-style-swap-that-saved-borderlands-1) puts the number at six directions. That figure comes from a retrospective sixteen years later, so treat it as recollection rather than a contemporaneous count; the style sheet itself is documented in the 2010 reporting.

Gearbox picked the safe option. In a market that was about to fill up with brown post-apocalyptic shooters, realism felt like the defensible choice. It was the wrong one.

## Why the realistic look stopped working

By October 2008 the team had two problems, and both are in GameSpot's account of the GDC talk.

The first was collision. Internal reviews by the "Gearbox Truth Team," a group of testers with psychology backgrounds, concluded that players found the art too similar to the newly released Fallout 3. id Software's Rage was in the same visual territory. Brian Martel, Gearbox's co-founder and chief creative officer, was blunt about it: "We didn't want to be considered a poor man's Rage." Adam May, a character modeler on the original and now art director on Borderlands 4, remembered the moment Fallout 3 was announced in the 2025 interview: "Oh crap. We're in the same visual space that they are."

The second was internal. The team called the old look its "Brown Period," and it was drifting away from the game underneath it. Vehicles could launch off ramps and catch enormous air. Enemies exploded. Bosses were absurd. The skill tree had accumulated workmanlike abilities that did nothing for the fantastical side of an RPG. "The jump height was the real trigger," Neumann said. A game that played like a cartoon was insisting on looking like a documentary.

Gearbox's own slides give the reasons in four lines. Slide 60 of the [GDC 2010 deck](https://media.gdcvault.com/gdc10/slides/Martel_Brian_Borderlands_and_the_11th_Hour_Art_Change.pdf): `Embrace fun!`, `Stand out from the crowd.`, `Break our plausibility shackles`, `Make a beautiful game.`

## The prototype nobody was supposed to see

The initial spark came from the art team, but the prototype was developed with the involvement of senior creative leadership. It was not simply a top-down mandate from management. In a [Kotaku interview published 12 June 2009](https://kotaku.com/the-new-borderlands-look-that-almost-wasnt-5288292/), Pitchford described a few artists complaining that games were all starting to look alike, looking at pictures of concept cars and asking why they never got to drive those in a game. His words: "So four of them got together and prototyped this new look out. Then they roped more people in to help them." He called it "like a subversive coup going on."

The 2025 interview adds the detail behind it. May sat next to concept artist Scott Kester, whose downtime doodles he describes as "illustrated graffiti." One sketch caught his eye; the character in it became Captain Flynt. Working with Kester, Brian Martel and art director Brian Cozzens, May helped turn that 2D look into 3D characters. When the group took the idea to Pitchford, they were up against their Alpha milestone.

By Pitchford's own 2025 account, he gave them two weeks and immediately regretted it:

> "It was, like, five guys with two weeks to go mess around with the look and the feel, and I immediately knew I made a mistake. I was like, 'S---. We're trying to get to Alpha, dude. There's a business here. I'm responsible for other people's money. …'"

He expected to kill it. He did not. "Two weeks go by, I go into the meeting and, looking at it, it's f---ing right; It's right," he recalled. "It feels right. And it's like everything we knew about what was wrong was confirmed when we felt it was right."

The 2009 interview tells the same turn without the elapsed time. Pitchford said there was a "million percent chance" he would shut the proposal down; the artists set up a presentation in a conference room anyway; seeing it moving, he said "holy crap" and "It looked like Borderlands but it's in a style I'd never seen before."

The slides confirm the secrecy. Slide 49: "That is why we hid in a closet and did a secret prototype." Slide 54 lists the stages of the `SECRET PROJECT` — getting buy-in, creating interest, fuelling passions, a snowball effect, "Getting Caught," and "Taking it to 2k!"

![Slide 49 of Brian Martel's GDC 2010 presentation, headed Becoming the Borderlands Art Director, ending with the line about hiding in a closet to build a secret prototype.](/img/gdc2010/gdc-slide-49-secret-prototype.png)

*Slide 49, GDC 2010. The "I" on this slide is Martel, a Gearbox co-founder — the deck frames the secret prototype as a decision taken inside senior creative leadership, not as a rebellion from below.*

What the prototype actually did is described in a [Destructoid piece from 16 April 2009](https://www.destructoid.com/borderlands-explains-its-not-cel-shaded-actually-art-style/), quoting Gearbox's technical explanation:

> "The new technique uses hand-drawn textures, scanned in and colored in Photoshop, combined with software that draws graphic novel-style outlines around characters and objects, sharpens shadows to look more like something an artist might create, and even draws lines on hills and inclines."

Character models were rebuilt with exaggerated proportions. That is a production pipeline, not a shader toggle — which is why the studio objected to the cel-shaded label in the first place.

## Seventy-five percent finished

Here is the part that makes the story unusual. GameSpot's GDC report puts the game at 75 percent complete when the decision was taken. Gearbox had no budget to start over and a release window it wanted to hit. Of time, money and quality, only quality was still movable.

Slide 7 of the deck sets the change inside the project's own timeline:

| Phase | Dates |
| :--- | :--- |
| Concept | Apr 05 – Oct 05 |
| Prototype | Nov 05 – Oct 06 |
| Development 1 | Nov 06 – Aug 07 |
| Development 2 | Sep 07 – Oct 08 |
| Final Push | Oct 08 – Oct 09 — "doubled team size, art style change, story and design finalized" |

![Slide 7 of Brian Martel's GDC 2010 presentation: the Borderlands development timeline, with art style change listed under Final Push.](/img/gdc2010/gdc-slide-07-timeline.png)

*Slide 7, GDC 2010. Reproduced from the [GDC Vault PDF](https://media.gdcvault.com/gdc10/slides/Martel_Brian_Borderlands_and_the_11th_Hour_Art_Change.pdf).*

That is a studio recording the overhaul in its own conference material, in the same phase as a doubling of the team. It is the strongest single item in the record, and it is dated.

The three figures in this section come from different places and should not be merged. **75 percent** is GameSpot's number from its report of the session; it is not printed on a Gearbox slide. The **Oct 08 – Oct 09** window is on slide 7 of the deck. **Two weeks** is Pitchford's recollection in 2025. The slides state no completion percentage.

There was still a publisher to convince. Pitchford flew from Gearbox's office in Plano, Texas to 2K's offices in Novato, California, and pitched the change to the marketing and publishing teams, including then-president Christoph Hartmann. There was pushback over deadlines, but he got full support. "If I didn't believe that was the case, something else would have happened," he said in 2025. The 2009 Kotaku interview gives an earlier, shorter version: he flew to 2K "in October," showed it first to Greg Gobbi, and "the meetings got bigger and bigger." Slide 59 records the studio's reaction to the answer: "They surprised us and actually LOVED it as much as we did. Oh crap! Now we have to make it!"

## What it cost

Approval was the easy part. Graeme Timmins, lead level designer on the original and creative director on Borderlands 4, described the aftermath in the 2025 interview:

> "We had already been working on the game for several years at that point, and not only did we change the art style, we basically threw out all of the levels – I think only Trash Coast and, like, one other level made it through – everything else, we remade basically from scratch. From January to, like, August or September of that year, all of the level designers … we rebuilt the whole game to match the new art style from that time."

His first reaction, when asked what he thought on hearing about the switch: "F---ing insane."

It cost people too. GameSpot reports that the original art director, disenchanted that her prior work was being discarded, eventually quit the project, left Gearbox and left the game industry altogether. May: "We lost a few people during that art transition, because we had a lot of people that were excited about doing something that looked pretty grounded and realistic."

The team saved time wherever it could. The Psycho enemy and the open-turreted runner vehicles survived largely intact. Technology was imported from Brothers in Arms: Hell's Highway. The dramatic shadows came from a piece of invented fiction — a stationary moon, giving one fixed light source in the sky. Claptrap began as a throwaway sketch produced by a low-level assignment in the JIRA project-management system. Game design was reworked alongside the visuals, with dull abilities scrapped in favour of things like "Healing bullets!" And Brick, added as a fourth Vault Hunter around this time, was the only one of the original four Vault Hunters to exist exclusively in the new art style.

The whole thing shipped in 2009.

## Two accounts of the prototype team

Two accounts of the same prototype are worth stating plainly rather than smoothing over.

The June 2009 interview, published while the game was still in development, says **four** artists started the prototype and then "roped more people in." The 2025 interview, sixteen years later, has Pitchford saying **"five guys with two weeks."** These are not necessarily contradictory — the contemporary account explicitly says the group grew — but they are different numbers from different kinds of source, and the two-week figure appears only in the recollection.

The more common error is to let the two weeks swallow the rest. Two weeks bought a prototype. The rebuild took months. Timmins puts it from January to August or September; the GDC timeline puts the art style change inside a phase running from October 2008 to October 2009. "Borderlands changed its entire art style in two weeks" is not a claim any of these sources supports.

There is a similar wobble on timing. The 2009 interview says Pitchford flew to 2K "in October"; the 2025 interview gives no month. Slide 59 says only that he went. Nothing here justifies stitching those into a single precise date.

## What the record does and does not prove

A word on the label, since it keeps attaching itself to this story.

Slide 53 of the GDC deck sets two things side by side: `FAKE CEL SHADING` and `BORDERLANDS SHADING (Concept Art Style)`. Gearbox was drawing a line between the technique most people were naming and what it had built. That is a studio's own classification in its own conference slides — good evidence for what Gearbox called the work, not a technical audit of every rendering path in the shipped game.

![Slide 53 of Brian Martel's GDC 2010 presentation: two versions of the same character model labelled FAKE CEL SHADING and BORDERLANDS SHADING (Concept Art Style).](/img/gdc2010/gdc-slide-53-naming.png)

*Slide 53, GDC 2010. Reproduced from the [GDC Vault PDF](https://media.gdcvault.com/gdc10/slides/Martel_Brian_Borderlands_and_the_11th_Hour_Art_Change.pdf).*

The contemporary press did not always follow that line. GameSpot's 2010 report describes the gamble as "betting on an art style close to cel-shading," while Kotaku's April 2009 headline was `Gearbox: "Borderlands Is Not Cel-Shaded"`, with Pitchford's email reply: "No, Borderlands is not cel-shaded — it's a gritty and serious world after all." A 2019 post attributed to Pitchford, preserved in a [PlayFront article](https://playfront.de/en/borderlands3-cel-shading-stil/) as an embedded tweet dated 7 September 2019, puts it more generously: "We always called it 'concept art style' or 'graphic novel art style' – I guess we were trying to be classy, but there is NOTHING wrong with 'comic book style'." That quote was read in the media transcription, not at the original post, so it sits one step further from the source.

None of it settles whether the finished game has any visual or technical resemblance to cel shading. It settles what the studio called the work, and when.

## Three layers of evidence

The material here is not all the same strength, and it is worth keeping them apart.

**Contemporaneous, 2009–2010.** Kotaku's April and June 2009 pieces, Destructoid's April 2009 technical description, GameSpot's report from the GDC 2010 session, and the 132-slide GDC deck itself. These were produced while the change was happening or within months of ship. The deck is the only item that is Gearbox's own unmediated conference material.

**Transcription, 2019.** A tweet quoted inside a third-party article. The wording is consistent with the 2009 position, but the primary post was not independently checked for this page.

**Retrospective, 2025.** Game Informer's interviews with Pitchford, May and Timmins, and the [80 Level summary](https://80.lv/articles/how-borderlands-devs-created-franchise-s-signature-art-style) that relays them. These supply the human detail — the doodle that became Captain Flynt, the closet, the flight to Novato, the levels that did not survive — from people who were there. They are also being recalled sixteen years on, which is why the numbers above get flagged rather than merged.

Within those layers, a developer's own words are not the same thing as the studio's own document. The distinction matters most for the technical description:

| Source | What it is | Weight here |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| GDC 2010 deck | Gearbox's own conference slides | Primary, unmediated |
| Pitchford email, April 2009 | Developer statement published by a news outlet | Primary quote, media-carried |
| Kotaku interview, June 2009 | Developer interview | Contemporaneous oral account |
| Destructoid, April 2009 | Press relaying a Gearbox technical explanation | Contemporaneous, one relay step |
| GameSpot, March 2010 | Report from the GDC session | Contemporaneous secondary |
| Game Informer, September 2025 | Developer interviews | Retrospective oral account |
