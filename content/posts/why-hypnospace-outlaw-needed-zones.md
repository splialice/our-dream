*Hypnospace Outlaw* was released on 12 March 2019 and asks the player to police a fictional 1999 internet. Its [official site](https://www.hypnospace.net/) describes the job as seeking out content "in violation of Hypnospace law by exploring a massive, detailed, fictional internet". The hard part was never the policing. It was making that mass of pages findable without handing the player a list of links.

In two interviews four years apart, creative lead Jay Tholen described how the team solved it. What follows is the documented chain: one part of the web was built for fidelity, and the part that made it playable was rebuilt after playtesters failed to understand it.

## The web started as a level select

The idea began in 2014 with *Hypnospace Enforcer*, a microgame made while Tholen was working on *Dropsy*. "In that game you're cruising down an 'information superhighway' chasing down internet users who had been reported for breaking rules," he told [RPS in 2023](https://www.rockpapershotgun.com/the-influences-and-surprising-origins-of-hypnospace-outlaw). Between levels, pages gave context about the accused. "Back then it was more of a glorified level select system, with the main gameplay occurring on the highway."

The [original Kickstarter campaign](https://www.kickstarter.com/projects/jaytholen/hypnospace-outlaw), created by Jay Tholen and run from 7 September to 4 October 2016, promised: "Pursue outlaws on the Hypnospace Highway." The same page paired that driving premise with an "arcane operating system" and virtual pets. That mode did not ship as planned. "We ran out of time to complete it as we had originally envisioned it by our release window but it wasn't removed, just repurposed and scoped down." Drawing on Brian Eno's oblique strategy *Emphasise the flaws*, Tholen put the unfinished thing into the fiction: "We ran out of time to develop it in real life, so why not make an in-game character also run out of time and release a half-finished version?" The "bad car game" became an in-joke among Hypnospace's characters. He added that "as development continued I realised that the appeal was in the web and the unfolding abilities of HypnOS."

That is a documented decision, not an inference from the interface: the simulated browser became the game because the other mode could not be finished.

## A directory that was not a mechanic

[Alex Wiltshire's 2019 interview](https://www.rockpapershotgun.com/2019/10/09/how-hypnospace-outlaws-1990s-internet-was-made) starts from the pre-Google web, when directories such as GeoCities' Neighborhoods sorted pages into Area51, TimesSquare and SunsetStrip. Tholen's reason for including a homepage directory was fidelity, not puzzle design: "It had nothing to do with the game design, it just had to be in there". He was 12 in 1999, the year the fictional network is set.

## The decision that produced the puzzles

Wiltshire reports that Tholen originally wanted the directory "set by the characters who created Hypnospace's pages" — users grouping their own sites by their own logic. Playtesters could not read it. "It loosened the rules on what players can expect to find in any given place," Tholen said.

So MerchantSoft, the in-game corporation, dictated zones with what RPS calls "a bland corporate logic": Goodtime Valley ("Remember the good old days?") and Teentopia Paradise. Players could recognise those immediately.

The consequence was not planned. Wiltshire writes that Tholen realised the change "naturally opened up possibilities for puzzles": if a corporation enforces categories, users will hide pages that do not fit them, and an enforcer has to go looking. Unlisted pages, password-protected sites and hidden communities follow from a filing system. A fix for navigation generated the game's central hiding-and-seeking structure.

## Three routes in, and a box testers did not trust

MerchantSoft's zones were not the only way in. Tags and clubs such as `HS_Goofs-n-Laughs` work like the webrings that bound early sites together: "Since there are some unlisted pages, it was almost another overlapping circle of things you can explore outside the MerchantSoft-made zones", Tholen said. There is also a text search box. He was nervous about it because testers ignored it — "They didn't trust that it functions like a real OS would." Others guessed keywords he had not anticipated: "People found out quite a few sequence-breaks, sometimes by accident, because they guess things to type that I hadn't thought of."

The team responded by gating pages behind story progress and the number of jobs taken, so unassigned zones could not be searched. Tholen's reason was sheer volume: "It's just super overwhelming," and "Even with the three or four zones you have at the beginning, people seemed to feel a little overwhelmed."

The same question — how a player finds anything inside a simulated computer — is treated as development history in [*The Roottrees Are Dead*](/posts/why-roottrees-search-was-simple.html/), where a deliberately simple search was later rebuilt into a research notebook.

## Pages before puzzles

For the first year and a half, RPS reports, the team made only one puzzle. "There was no consideration for what the puzzles were going to be yet, or what information you needed to know," Tholen said. "We just made a bunch of content first. It's probably not advisable to do that!" Links between pages were then built backwards out of relationships between their authors, an approach he compared to digging through the GeoCities archive: "It felt like archaeology, and also a game."

By [InterfaceX's definition](/posts/what-are-fake-os-games-called.html/), this is why *Hypnospace Outlaw* belongs in the Fake OS conversation: the simulated browser is not a frame around the game, it is where the game happens. That is an editorial reading of the definition, not a label the developers claimed.

## What the reception adds

Reviews describe how that fictional web landed, not how it was designed. [PC Gamer called the game](https://www.pcgamer.com/hypnospace-outlaw-review/) "A satisfying detective adventure based around a weird and wonderfully imaginative retro internet." A [Steam user review](https://steamcommunity.com/app/844590/reviews/?browsefilter=toprated) by Foymic reads: "The Internet isn't dumb. It's just full of human beings, and human beings are beautiful." That is a player's reading of the people behind the pages — neither line is a developer statement about the directory.

## After 2019

The planned sequel belongs to a different history. Tholen announced its cancellation in a [video titled "Dreamsettler is canceled"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uH3UbgEWBxg) in June 2025, and [his own games page](https://www.jaytholen.net/jt-games.html) records the project as "cancelled June 2025". Those are first-party records of a 2025 decision; they are not evidence for the 2019 zoning decisions described above.
