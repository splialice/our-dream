Akihiro Ishihara directed the *Idolm@ster* series. His new visual novel looks like a bright idol game — six cute girls, a hero called the Messiah, a domed city. Then it takes a sharp turn into something far stranger and more philosophical.

And Ishihara says that is exactly the point: he made it for players around the world, but refused to smooth off its Japanese edges to get there.

## A Messiah who wakes up amnesiac

*Valkyrie Tune: Synthesis of Souls* comes from Frontwing and Good Smile Company, and has an unusual origin: it expands Good Smile's bishōjo plastic model franchise into a game. You play Rei, a young man who wakes from years of cryosleep in the domed city of Initium with no memory. A group of women greet him as the "Messiah."

At first, the game lets players enjoy a seemingly peaceful life with the six girls. But Ishihara says the story gradually introduces stranger questions: *Is this world truly the future? And who are these girls, really?*

> "While the early chapters depict a relatively calm everyday life, it's precisely because of that sense of security that the later developments leave a stronger impression. Questions such as 'Is this world truly the future?' and 'Who are these girls, really?' begin to surface, linking directly with the player's own emotions."

That is the hook. The game's marketing calls it a "sci-fi romance suspense" story, and Ishihara describes a work that builds "a sense of unease about the world" quietly and gradually. Think less "idol dating sim," more "idol dating sim that is quietly asking you unsettling questions."

## "We wouldn't deliberately water it down"

Ishihara spoke to AUTOMATON twice before release, and the clearest theme was his refusal to make the game more "global" by making it less Japanese.

> "Even with overseas expansion in mind, we wouldn't deliberately 'water down' the game's Japanese expression. Rather, I believe this game can offer a fresh experience to overseas players precisely because it is based on Japanese aesthetics and character presentation."

That is a specific argument, not a slogan. His claim is that the very qualities an overseas audience might find unfamiliar — the character styling, the emotional directness — are what make the game worth importing. He described the target player as anyone "interested in ethical and philosophical subjects, regardless of where they are from," especially those who like story-driven games and value emotional connection with characters. "This is a game made for gamers," he said.

He framed the goal as delivering an experience of "interacting with emotions," crossing language and cultural barriers: "I would be happy if the time spent with the girls would leave the players with something to remember inside of their hearts."

This philosophy also shapes how he approaches the heroines. Ishihara says he wants to portray characters "not as objects to be consumed, but as beings with whom emotions are shared." Rather than placing the player in a position of cheering the characters on, the game emphasises spending time alongside them at a close distance — going through both the good and the hard parts together. It is a different idea of what a player is to a cast: not an audience, but a companion.

## Cute on the surface, strange underneath

This is what makes the game interesting even if you never play it. Its public image — plastic-model heroines, bright key art, a music theme — points one way. Its actual pitch points another: an amnesiac "Messiah," a city where reality feels off, and a story that reportedly turns serious and philosophical.

The Steam page now describes an "amnesiac protagonist and six stunning heroines, whose intertwining serenade of love, sorrow, and fateful encounters calls forth a reboot for the future." Love and sorrow in the same sentence is close to the whole design in miniature.

## What happened after release

Those interviews were published in February 2026, before release. The game launched on 22 July 2026 for PC and Switch. By November it held a "Very Positive" rating on Steam — around 80% positive from a few dozen reviews. A small audience, but a warm one.

That matters because "we won't water it down for overseas players" is easy to say before launch. The more interesting question was whether anyone overseas would actually want it. The early reception is encouraging, if still very small. With only a few dozen Steam reviews, it is far too soon to know whether Ishihara's bet will pay off commercially. But at least the game has not needed to abandon its Japanese identity to find some overseas players.

## Why this is worth reading

The question a casual reader can follow: **why would a visual novel advertise that it refuses to change itself for foreign players?** The answer is a bet about what sells — that specific, unpolished cultural character travels better than something designed to be universally legible.

Ishihara is not arguing that overseas players need to be educated. His argument challenges an assumption that publishers may be making about overseas players: that Japanese games need to become less Japanese to travel internationally. Whether the game's small but positive reception proves him right is another matter; it is at least a more interesting bet than the usual "global appeal" playbook.
