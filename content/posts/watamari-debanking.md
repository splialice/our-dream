A yuri visual novel was indefinitely delayed — not by a bad review or a store policy, but by a bank's anti-money-laundering checks.

In 2026, developer Takii Noame saw revenue from past Steam games arrive in a lump after a sale. The bank's automated systems flagged it. Weeks later, *Watamari Part 2 – A Fake Marriage?* was indefinitely delayed, and the developer was telling other creators that preparing the paperwork "is completely pointless."

## How a sale turned into a crisis

Takii Noame makes yuri visual novels for an indie studio. In a Note post translated by AUTOMATON, they described the sequence: a Steam sale brought in a sudden influx of revenue, the bank's checks flagged it as suspected money laundering, and the bank then accused them of violating its terms.

The bank's objection, according to Takii, concerned adult-content patches associated with earlier yuri titles. The important distinction is that Watamari itself is an all-ages series. The sequel was delayed because of the developer's payment problems, not because the game itself contained adult content. Releasing external patches that restore adult content is a common practice for games on Steam.

The practical fallout was not theoretical. Takii wrote:

> "This is a recipe for anyone who was happily making a new yuri game, only to have their Steam sales blocked by their bank for some reason, completely throwing their schedule into disarray near the end of development and leaving them feeling like they're going to throw up."

Even asking the publisher to send only all-ages revenue through Japan Post Bank was rejected, without a clear reason given. The developer had to reshuffle contractors and apologise to everyone on the schedule:

> "I had to tear apart the schedules of all the contractors whose availability I'd painstakingly coordinated, apologize to everyone who had been working with me to keep things on track, and reschedule everything."

## The fix the developer hated

Takii's advice to other creators is blunt. After submitting a "huge amount of documentation," they concluded:

> "Spending time preparing all those documents is completely pointless."

The problem was only resolved by changing banks and giving up the adult patches for past games. The developer did not defend that as a good outcome. They expressed sympathy for creators whose work is entirely adult-focused: "all you can do is pray."

On 4 October 2026, development resumed, with a new release date to be announced. At the time of that post, the game had more than 15,000 Steam wishlists. It is a yuri visual novel in which the player acts as an "angel of love," helping the heroine Nene Yakumo guide other couples toward relationships. As of 20 November 2026, the Steam page still lists only a 2026 release year, with no fixed date.

## Why this is a bigger story than one game

The hook is unusual enough to carry the piece: **a game was delayed because a bank refused to process the transfers carrying its player revenue.** The deeper subject is how payment infrastructure shapes what small studios can make. This is not a platform ban or a store rating; it is a downstream financial decision by the bank, not a freeze imposed by Steam.

Adult content is part of the causal chain here, so it cannot be deleted from the account. But the article is not about the patches or their content. It is about a developer losing months of work because a bank's risk model did not distinguish an all-ages yuri game from the patches attached to it.

## Evidence boundaries

This article follows Takii Noame's original Japanese Note post and AUTOMATON WEST's reports from August and October 2026, plus the game's Steam page. Steam sold the games and the publisher held the revenue; the bank refused to process the transfers.
