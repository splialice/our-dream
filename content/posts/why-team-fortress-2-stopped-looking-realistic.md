The Team Fortress 2 Valve showed in 1999 was a realistic military shooter. The Team Fortress 2 that shipped in October 2007 looked like a 1930s magazine advertisement: heavy silhouettes, painted shadows, nine characters you could identify from across the map. The change between those two versions is one of the best-documented art-direction decisions in games, because Valve wrote down why it made the call while it was making it. A six-page conference paper, a 57-slide deck, a three-and-a-half-minute developer video and two interviews from 2007 and 2008 all survive.

That makes it a useful contrast with [Borderlands](/posts/borderlands-11th-hour-art-change.html/), where the decision came with the project roughly three-quarters finished and had to be pieced together from a 2010 conference talk, a contemporaneous report and interviews sixteen years later. In TF2's case the design rationale was published before most people had played the game. What did not survive is the date of the decision itself.

## What the 1999 version looked like

Valve hired the Team Fortress mod team in 1998 and announced a sequel as a Half-Life add-on. By E3 1999 the project was being shown publicly as `Team Fortress 2: Brotherhood of Arms`, and it looked like a contemporary military shooter: realistic visuals, a Commander class, parachute drops, networked voice chat. The six years that followed produced very little substantial public information — a second delay in 2000, a switch to the Source engine, and regular appearances on Wired's vaporware list. It was not total silence: in [a HomeLan interview reproduced by IGN in October 2004](https://www.ign.com/articles/2004/10/19/valve-qa-3), Valve's Doug Lombardi said "TF2 is not dead at all", confirmed the move to Source and promised more news after Half-Life 2 shipped. Valve reintroduced the game in July 2006 with the stylised look, and shipped it on 10 October 2007 as part of The Orange Box.

That timeline is widely repeated, but it is a secondary account: the dates come from [Wikipedia's development section](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Team_Fortress_2) and the reporting it cites, not from a Valve project log. It tells you that the realistic version was shown and that the illustrated version shipped. It does not tell you when, or at whose desk, the first was abandoned.

## More than one discarded direction

The 1999 and 2006 images invite a clean before-and-after story. The people who made the game describe something messier. In an [interview published by Game Developer on 7 December 2007](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/valve-s-francke-game-art-direction-in-its-infancy-), after his talk at the Lyon Game Developers Conference, art director Moby Francke said:

> "I came to Valve in 2002 and Team Fortress 2 had gone through, I think, about four different iterations, four different art styles at that time, from 1999. So I came in at the tail end of one of their other failed art experiments."

He added that he "basically brought to them the characters, the shapes, and how we could make this work as a class-based multiplayer game", and called the result "art by consensus": "So for Team Fortress, I had to continually say why we're doing this. Silhouettes have to be this way. Colors, through gameplay and playtesting, we were able to achieve." A secondary account on [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Team_Fortress_2) records Robin Walker saying much the same in March 2007 — that Valve built "three or four different versions" before settling on the final design.

Valve's own later material confirms that the illustrated look was not the first alternative tried. In [A Grim Bloody Fable](https://www.teamfortress.com/post.php?id=1783), a post on the official TF2 blog dated 28 August 2008, Dhabih Eng wrote that "soon after we decided that we were going to start a new, more stylized direction for TF2", one of the first ideas was to make the world look like claymation. It was short-lived:

> "A quick set of concepts were made to test the idea but we quickly realized that though the novelty of such a direction would be interesting, it didn't fit some of our higher level goals in terms of what kind of product we were trying to deliver. However, we did want to hold onto this concept of simulating real world artistic materials in the game and this led to the more painterly look which eventually shipped with the product."

The same post shows the Demoman's silhouette growing more distinct across successive concepts because "a stronger emphasis was simultaneously being placed on readability of the classes". And Jason Mitchell's [82-slide GDC 2008 deck](https://steamcdn-a.akamaihd.net/apps/valve/2008/GDC2008_StylizationWithAPurpose_TF2.pdf), `Stylization with a Purpose: The Illustrative World of Team Fortress 2`, opens with a visual history that sets the original Team Fortress mod, the "Initial Team Fortress 2" and the shipped game side by side.

None of this dates the moment the Leyendecker direction was adopted, and the 2007 interview does not say it was already in place when Francke arrived in 2002. What these sources do establish is that the move away from realism ran through several abandoned versions — not a single decision that can be pinned to one afternoon.

## The playtest that broke it

The clearest story about why the realistic look failed comes from a talk Moby Francke, TF2's art director, gave at Microsoft's Gamefest in 2008. [Christian Nutt and Michael Zenke reported it for Game Developer on 29 July 2008](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/art/in-depth-how-valve-makes-art-to-enhance-gameplay). Francke said the sequel had started out "with a realistic military style, both in visual and gameplay terms", and then described what happened when that met the actual game:

> "TF is over-the-top from a gameplay perspective - you can rocket jump, you can magically heal people. They started to run into problems during play testing."

The example he gave is small and specific. There was a tiny medic patch on the sleeve of the Medic, and in playtesting it was nearly impossible to tell Medics apart from other players. A realistic uniform gave the eye too little to work with at the speed the game was played. The report says the designers concluded they should "aim to match the game's look to the gameplay", and quotes Francke:

> "Due to this high-paced, very stylized gameplay, we thought of going for something more unique, something that's more shape driven, color-driven."
>
> "Readability was a very important thing - we wanted the characters to stand out in the world, and the ability to tell who is who. From a branding perspective, TF2 differentiates itself from other games quite easily."

Two cautions belong next to that quotation. It is a media report of a conference talk given after release, so it is retrospective developer testimony rather than a document written at the moment of the decision. And the patch anecdote explains a design problem; it is not a dated account of a meeting where the art direction was formally changed. Nothing in the surviving record pins that moment to a year.

## Three reasons, written down in 2007

What Valve did publish in 2007 was unusually direct. Jason Mitchell, Moby Francke and Dhabih Eng — all three at Valve — presented `Illustrative Rendering in Team Fortress 2` at the fifth International Symposium on Non-Photorealistic Animation and Rendering (NPAR 2007), with a related SIGGRAPH 2007 course. The [six-page paper](https://steamcdn-a.akamaihd.net/apps/valve/2007/NPAR07_IllustrativeRenderingInTeamFortress2.pdf) and the [57-slide deck](https://steamcdn-a.akamaihd.net/apps/valve/2007/NPAR07_IllustrativeRenderingInTeamFortress2_Slides.pdf) are still hosted on Valve's own CDN. Slide 3 lists the motives as three bullet points:

> "Differentiation - multiplayer combat games tend to embrace a contemporary photorealistic look"
>
> "Gameplay - Team Fortress has always featured cartoonish, over-the-top situations"
>
> "Readability - Class differentiation is the core of Team Fortress 2, hence we needed to be able to clearly differentiate classes visually"

The paper's introduction says the same thing in prose: "we sought to explicitly differentiate ourselves from other multiplayer deathmatch games which typically embrace a modern photorealistic look", and "art and technology choices combine to support stylistic goals and gameplay constraints". It also adds a detail the slides leave out — the look is grounded in "early 20th century commercial illustration with 1960s industrial design elements".

This is a different kind of evidence from an interview. It is a studio explaining its reasoning to an academic conference, in a dated document, in the same year the game shipped. Nobody was asking Valve to justify a change of direction; the authors were describing a rendering pipeline. That is why the three reasons matter: differentiation, gameplay and readability were not invented afterwards to defend a look. A year later, Mitchell's GDC 2008 deck restated the same list with slightly different labels — "Gameplay", "Readability" and "Branding". The differentiation argument had become a branding argument. Both are Valve's own words; the shift is a reminder that even primary sources are snapshots of how a team described its work at a particular moment.

## A style built to be read

The paper's abstract is explicit that the shading was not decoration. Its techniques were "designed to quickly convey geometric information using rim highlights as well as variation in luminance and hue, so that game players are consistently able to visually 'read' the scene and identify other players in a variety of lighting conditions". Slide 22 breaks that into what the team called a visual "read hierarchy":

| Level | Question | Visual signal |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Team | Friend or foe? | Colour |
| Class | Run or attack? | Distinctive silhouettes, body proportions, shoes, hats and clothing folds |
| Selected weapon | "What's he packin'?" | Highest contrast at chest level, where the weapon is held; gradient from dark feet to light chest |

The character work followed the same rule. The paper says the nine silhouettes "were carefully designed to be very distinct from one another", and that showing the characters "only in silhouette with no internal shading at all" was used to validate the designs during the concept phase. Body proportions, weapons, footwear, hats and clothing folds were all drawn to give each class a unique outline. Interior clothing folds were chosen to echo the silhouette shapes.

The visual vocabulary came from commercial illustrators, not from other games. The introduction names J. C. Leyendecker, Dean Cornwell and Norman Rockwell, and the deck has a slide devoted to the conventions it took from Leyendecker:

> "Shading obeys a warm-to-cool hue shift. Shadows go to cool, not black"
>
> "Silhouettes are often emphasized with rim highlights rather than dark outlines"

Francke put the same idea in plainer language in a [GamesIndustry.biz interview published on 20 December 2007](https://www.gamesindustry.biz/to-be-franke), conducted at GDC Lyon with Matt Martin:

> "It's such a fast-paced game so you don't want to spend time worrying about the little things. Players need to get it straight away and we've done that with the silhouette and then the colours of each character."

Asked about the friction between realistic artwork and unrealistic gameplay, he answered: "It completely doesn't work." He used the Medic's healing beam as the example — both teams need to understand what the beam means — and then said, "It's not just branding and a pretty picture, it's also a language by which people can communicate." In the same interview he called the visual branding "essential", because a TF2 screenshot "has to say a thousand different things" at a time when other shooters looked alike.

So the look was doing three jobs at once: making the game recognisable on a shelf, making it possible to read a fight, and making an exaggerated game feel like one visual system. The 2007 paper is the source for the first two; the interviews supply the vocabulary of branding and language.

## The cel-shaded label, again

Players have called TF2 cel-shaded for years. Valve's own people ruled that out at Gamefest, in a line worth keeping whole:

> "We didn't want to go the cel-shaded route, we wanted to go more true to the way that lighting occurs."

That sentence comes from the 2008 Game Developer report of Francke's talk, not from the NPAR paper. In the paper, "cel-shaded" appears only in the related-work section, where the authors describe other researchers mapping a Lambertian term to a small set of colours with a hard transition. Valve does not use the word for its own work there, and does not stop to deny it either. Treating the paper as an official denial would be a misreading.

The distinction is the same one that runs through [Is Borderlands Cel Shaded?](/posts/is-borderlands-cel-shaded.html/) and [What Is the Borderlands Art Style Called?](/posts/borderlands-art-style.html/): a dark outline or a flattened shadow does not establish that a game uses a cel-shading pipeline. In TF2 the team explicitly used rim highlights instead of dark outlines, warm-to-cool hue shifts instead of black shadows, and a mix of photorealistic and non-photorealistic shading. In Borderlands, Gearbox rejected the label while describing hand-drawn textures and software-generated graphic-novel outlines. Two studios, one repeated misnomer, two quite different pipelines.

Four limits are worth stating plainly. No source in this set dates the meeting where the realistic version was dropped; the 1999 and 2006 reveals bracket the change but do not locate it. The NPAR paper is a technical description of a finished style, not a history of the decision. The most vivid anecdote — the tiny medic patch — is a developer's recollection in a talk reported a year after release: good evidence for the design problem, weaker evidence for the chronology. And both 2007 interviews are recollections too; Francke's "about four different iterations" is explicitly an estimate, not a project log. The 2008 blog post and the conference decks are dated, but none of them dates the decision itself.

[Our earlier comparison piece](/posts/tf2-borderlands-before-the-makeover.html/) recorded that the ACM page for the paper returned 403 and the original Valve-hosted PDF returned 404 during research on 9 November 2026. That was true of those two addresses, but it is not the end of the paper: the Official TF2 Wiki's Resources section still points to Valve's own copies on its Steam CDN, and both the paper and the slide deck were downloaded and read in full for this article. The DOI ([10.1145/1274871.1274883](https://doi.org/10.1145/1274871.1274883)) remains a valid citation; the primary documents are simply somewhere else.

## Where this sits beside the Borderlands articles

Both games abandoned a realistic look because the gameplay had outgrown it. Both were also called cel-shaded by players and not by their makers. They differ in shape as well as in their records: Borderlands changed direction late and rebuilt much of a nearly finished game, while TF2's move away from realism was spread across several abandoned versions over years. The records, though, point in opposite directions.

TF2 has an unusually strong contemporary answer to "why does it look like this": a paper, a slide deck and a video from 2007, plus an interview from the same year. What it lacks is a dated account of the switch — no talk titled "the 11th-hour style change", no timeline slide marking the decision, no reported percentage of completion. Borderlands has almost the reverse: a GDC talk with a development timeline, a GameSpot report from the session giving 75 percent completion, and 2025 interviews naming the people and the two-week prototype — but its naming dispute ran for a decade and the studio's own term, `Concept Art Style`, only ever appeared in statements and slides.

Reading them together is the point of [the evidence comparison](/posts/tf2-borderlands-before-the-makeover.html/): a claim about an art makeover is only as good as the material left behind, and the material differs in kind, not just in quantity. TF2 lets you reconstruct an argument. Borderlands lets you reconstruct a sequence of events. Neither gives you everything.
