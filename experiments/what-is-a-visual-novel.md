## Short answer

A visual novel is a story game built around reading: text, character art and sound carry the narrative, and choices may branch it. It names a form, not a fixed set of mechanics. That is why Danganronpa, Persona and Omori can all be called visual novels in conversation while their own store pages list them as an Adventure, an RPG and an RPG.

## The explanation that keeps failing

Ask someone to explain a visual novel to a friend who has never played one, and the first attempt is usually a bad one. "Think of a script with pictures" sounds reasonable until the friend asks what that means.

The explanations that work take the opposite route. They reach for a game the other person already knows: it is like a book with music and character art; it is like *Persona* with the RPG systems removed; it is like *Danganronpa* or *Ace Attorney* with most of the gameplay taken away. These are approximations, not definitions. But each one points at something real.

Notice what is happening. Nobody explains the form in the abstract. Every useful answer borrows a familiar game. That is a clue about what "visual novel" means: it is a family resemblance, not a checklist.

## What the form actually is

[VNPaths](https://vnpaths.com/what-is-a-visual-novel/) puts it plainly: "The single most important thing to understand about visual novels is that they are, at heart, a reading experience." Its definition is a story delivered on a screen, combining prose-like depth with illustrated art and music, with interactivity that comes mainly from choices that branch the narrative.

Wikipedia's entry describes the genre as "a video game genre of Japanese origin focused on presenting a story," with progress made through "clicking, tapping or pressing a button." Both descriptions circle the same thing: the player's main activity is reading, looking and listening, not aiming, jumping or managing resources.

That does not mean nothing happens. There may be routes, affection meters, investigations or dialogue choices. It means those systems sit around a text-driven core rather than replacing it.

## Where the name came from

The term is younger than the form. According to the current [Wikipedia compilation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visual_novel), the earliest commercial use commonly cited for the term "visual novel" is Leaf's *Shizuku* (1996), which was promoted as part of a "Visual Novel Series." The next year, Tactics used the word during the development and release of *Moon* (1997). The team behind *Moon* and *One* later formed Key, whose *Kanon* (1999) is credited with establishing the market.

None of this makes *Shizuku* the first game that fits a modern definition of the form. An early use of a name and the first example of a thing are different claims, and only the first one is being made here.

That last step is worth hedging. The same entry quotes journalist Koji Fukuyama saying he believed Leaf, Tactics and Key may have popularised the term — while openly noting that he lacked empirical data. This is a plausible published account, not a settled history. The early Japanese magazines behind it were not checked directly for this article, so the route from those games to the word should be read as the current published explanation.

The useful part is the sequence. Games with this shape existed before the label settled. Arguments about whether a game "counts" are therefore arguments about boundaries, not about a rulebook someone wrote first.

## Three games, three answers

Here is where the confusion becomes concrete. All three games get called visual novels in ordinary conversation. Their own store pages tell a more precise story.

### Danganronpa: yes in practice, Adventure on the label

Steam lists *Danganronpa: Trigger Happy Havoc* with the official genre **Adventure**. Its first popular user tag is **Visual Novel**. That gap is small but real: the store's classification is Adventure; the audience's is Visual Novel.

There is also a first-party clue. In August 2023, Spike Chunsoft put four *Danganronpa* titles into its official [Steam Visual Novel Fest sale](https://www.spike-chunsoft.com?p=6535/), alongside *Steins;Gate*, *CHAOS;HEAD* and *Zero Escape*. A sale category is marketing, not a formal taxonomy — but it shows the publisher was comfortable selling these games under the visual novel label.

So the honest answer is: *Danganronpa* is built around reading dialogue, portraits and choices, and its publisher has grouped it with visual novels. Its store genre is Adventure because its class trials and investigations are not incidental. Both statements can be true.

### Persona: uses visual-novel presentation, remains an RPG

Steam lists *Persona 5 Royal* as **RPG**. Its popular tags include JRPG, Story Rich and Dating Sim — but not Visual Novel.

This is the idea behind the "like Persona, but without the RPG" shorthand. The social sections look and read like a visual novel: text scenes, portraits, relationship choices. Then the game switches to dungeons, turn-based combat and a calendar. Remove those systems and you would have something novel-shaped; keep them and the game is an RPG that uses visual-novel presentation for part of its story.

Calling *Persona* a visual novel is understandable shorthand for that presentation. Calling it only a visual novel loses the systems that take up most of its play time.

### Omori: visual-novel storytelling inside an RPG

Steam lists *OMORI* with the official genres **Adventure, Indie, RPG**. Its popular tags include Psychological Horror, Story Rich and Turn-Based — again, no Visual Novel tag.

It was made in RPG Maker and has party combat and exploration. Its quieter scenes — reading what characters say, following a trail of clues through Headspace and the real world — use the same screen grammar as a visual novel. That is why players reach for the word. But the form is one layer of the game, not the whole container.

## The test that actually works

Stop asking "is game X a visual novel?" and ask three separate questions instead.

| Question | What it tells you |
| :--- | :--- |
| How is the story presented? | Text, portraits, backgrounds and choices point to the visual novel form. |
| What does the player actually do? | Combat, calendars and exploration point to Adventure, RPG or Dating Sim. |
| What does the publisher call it? | The store genre is a label for shelving, not a ruling on the form. |

A game can score "yes" on the first row and "no" on the second. That is not a contradiction. It is why *Danganronpa*, *Persona* and *Omori* all attract the same word.

[NVL vs ADV](/posts/nvl-vs-adv.html/) looks at two text layouts inside the form, and [What Is a Kinetic Novel?](/posts/kinetic-novel-meaning.html/) explains the linear case with no branching choices. [What Is an Otome Game?](/posts/what-is-an-otome-game.html/) covers the label that answers a different question again: who the romance is for.

## The questions people actually ask

**Is a visual novel just a book with pictures?**
Think of it as an illustrated, interactive story: you read the dialogue, see the characters and scenes, and sometimes make choices that change what happens next. Many also use music and voice acting, but neither is required.

**Do all visual novels have choices?**
No. A kinetic novel is deliberately linear, with no branching or alternate endings. See the dedicated guide linked above.

**Are visual novels the same as dating sims?**
No. A dating sim builds romance through stats, schedules and affection meters; a visual novel can tell a romance without them. [The full boundary is here](/posts/visual-novel-vs-dating-sim.html/).

**Does a visual novel have to look like anime?**
No. Anime-style art is common but not part of the definition; the form is about how a story is delivered.

**Why do people call Persona and Omori visual novels then?**
Because their dialogue scenes use the form. That describes a layer of those games, not their only genre.
