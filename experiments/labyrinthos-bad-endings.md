A studio asked its players what they expected from its games. More than 60% said they expected to find bad endings in them. About 40% said they hoped for pure love stories instead. The studio did not pick one side and delete the other; it said both matter.

That answer comes from developer Astronauts in a 2026 interview about *LABYRINTHOS: The Depths Want You*. It is one of the more interesting numbers in recent visual-novel-adjacent game coverage because it is not a market statistic. It is a studio's own user-survey result, and the studio treats it as a live design constraint — one it has no intention of resolving in favour of a single answer.

## The number, with its limits

Astronauts put it this way:

> "'Bad ends' have always been a defining feature of our games, and if you have a look at user surveys, you'll see that over 60% of our players expect to see 'bad ends' in our games."

Then the other half:

> "On the other hand, about 40% of players are hoping to see 'pure love stories' rather than 'bad ends.' Neither of these options is the absolutely correct answer, and we intend to pay close attention to the demands of our players."

This is not "60% of visual novel players love suffering." It is Astronauts describing **their own players** and **their own games**. The scope matters, and so does the verb: *expecting* bad endings to be part of a studio's games is not the same as *wanting* a favourite character to suffer. Nor does the interview establish a clean 60/40 split between two mutually exclusive camps; it reports two separate survey answers. The interesting part is not the percentage itself but what the studio does with it: it keeps both expectations in the room rather than pretending one group is wrong.

Astronauts also said the ratio could change the future:

> "If this ratio significantly shifts, it may impact how we create our future titles going forward."

That turns a fan preference into a production signal.

## Why bad endings are a feature, not a punishment

Astronauts describes games as an active medium where players make decisions and then live with multiple outcomes. In that framing, a bad ending is not just a cruel trick. It is proof that choices have weight.

> "A unique appeal of video games, which doesn't exist within other media, is that they depict multiple different endings, like a 'good end' and a 'bad end,' and allow the player to experience each one for themselves."

At the same time, the studio understands why someone would not want that:

> "We completely understand that some people may feel distressed by the mere possibility of their favorite character meeting a bad end."

That is the real tension. One group plays to see what choices cost. Another plays because the possibility of losing a character hurts. If you only write “bad endings are meaningful,” you miss half the design problem.

## LABYRINTHOS is not a plain visual novel

The interview is not only about endings. Astronauts describes a brand that has never stayed inside one format:

> "We develop gameplay-heavy titles like LABYRINTHOS: The Depths Want You and the aforementioned DEMONION, as well as story-driven visual novels like the Kyokugen Chikan Tokuiten series."

Shiravune, the English publisher, explains why this game was chosen as an entry point:

> "Since it incorporates roguelite and dungeon exploration elements, the defining feature of Labyrinthos, other than the story and characters, is that the gameplay requires players to think strategically as they progress."

![LABYRINTHOS battle screen](/img/editorial/labyrinthos-03.jpg)

*Official screenshot from the LABYRINTHOS Steam page. © Astronauts / Shiravune.*

So the bad-ending question sits inside a larger production question. Astronauts says entertainment has become extremely diverse and that users now tend to "prioritize value for money and time efficiency." On top of that, the domestic market is shrinking, and expanding overseas raises the cost of localising a large volume of text. Under those conditions, the studio says, continuing to make games in the old full-price format and scale is getting harder. That is what the interview argues — a specific pressure on a specific kind of game, not a claim that the whole market has demanded faster, cheaper products.

Astronauts' answer is variety. The studio says sticking to one genre might make stable sales easier, but trying many genres keeps creators from getting stuck. It also admits that small studios cannot beat large ones at the "widely-supported, optimal answer." Instead:

> "We're aiming to make niche and unique works that will make a select group of people feel, 'This is exactly the game I wanted to play.'"

That is the same spirit as the two survey answers. It is not about finding the single correct audience. It is about serving a specific one honestly.

## Why this is a good story

The hook is simple: **a studio's own survey says its fans disagree about bad endings, and the studio refuses to ignore either group.** That is easy to understand without prior genre knowledge.

But the article has more underneath. It connects a player-survey number to a larger production strategy: small studio, niche audience, gameplay experimentation, overseas localisation costs, and the changing market for Japanese bishoujo games. Adult content is part of the category background; it is not the point of the story.

## Evidence boundaries

The over-60% and about-40% figures are quoted from Astronauts in AUTOMATON's October 2026 interview. They are not independently verified survey data, are reported as two separate answers rather than one two-option poll, and should not be read as a strict 60/40 split or generalised beyond Astronauts' own players. "Expecting" bad endings is not the same as wanting a preferred character to suffer. The screenshots are official Steam store images used for editorial identification and commentary.
