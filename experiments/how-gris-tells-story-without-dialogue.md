GRIS asks players to follow a story without line-by-line dialogue. The useful answer is not a hidden plot key but the set of visual channels Nomada Studio actually described: a colour script, statues, and camera and level cues.

## Short answer

Nomada's interviews do not offer a scene-by-scene decoding of the story. They describe three working channels: a colour script that marks story and character progression, statues that carry a large part of the exposition, and level design plus camera cues that tell the player where to go. The studio also simplified Gris because extreme zoom-outs and animation time constrained the design.

## What the developers actually said

In a [December 2018 interview](https://intothespine.com/2018/12/12/interview-nomada-studio-on-gris-inspiration-aspiration-and-aesthetic), Nomada Studio described GRIS as "a hopeful young girl lost in a strange, beautiful world devoid of color, struggling to cope with a recent tragedy." That is the starting point: the player is given a state of loss, not a narrator explaining it.

The same interview says the dress was given a "mutable" quality because the character needed to change as she advanced. That is a visual way of tracking growth without stopping the game for dialogue. Our [GRIS art style](/posts/gris-art-style.html/) article covers how that idea fits the wider illustrated look.

## Colour is the structure

Roger Mendoza told [The Verge](https://www.theverge.com/2018/12/20/18148805/gris-nintendo-switch-steam-art-animation) that colour does narrative work: "The colors mark the progression of the story and the characters. That's why at the beginning we did a color script to see the general palette of each area to make sure it worked well with the story."

That is more specific than saying the game "uses colour beautifully." A colour script was planned early, area by area, so the palette could carry changes in story and character state. The visual progression is therefore structural, not decorative.

This is also the main reason GRIS and [Journey](/posts/journey-art-style.html/) sit naturally together. Journey's developers described colour as an emotional arc; Nomada named Journey's minimalism as one of its references, then used colour to mark progression in its own game.

## Statues carry the explicit story

Mendoza's clearest sentence about exposition is: "We tell a big part of the story through the statues while relying heavily on the colors." The statues are not background sculpture in this account. They are one of the places where the game puts information the player is meant to read.

That division matters: colour tracks state and progression; statues carry a larger share of the explicit story. Treating every image as a coded line of dialogue goes beyond what the developers said.

## Readability is part of the storytelling

A story told visually only works if players can read it. Mendoza said: "It was also a struggle trying to find the balance between art and readability," and added: "You always want to make sure every frame is beautiful, but at the same time don't want to confuse the player. There was a lot of iteration on the level design and using visual and camera cues to make sure the player always knows where to go."

The character design follows the same rule. An earlier version showed the colours unlocked on the dress itself, but the team simplified it because it "didn't work well with extreme zoom outs" and was "much more difficult and time-consuming to animate." In other words, the visual story had to survive camera distance and production limits, not just look good in a concept image. Our [Why Game Characters Need Distinct Silhouettes](/posts/why-game-characters-need-distinct-silhouettes.html/) piece covers the same readability problem from the character side.

## What the sources do not settle

These two interviews explain the channels Nomada used. They do not provide an official scene-by-scene decoding, and they do not settle fan interpretations about grief, family or specific symbols. The Iwagumi aquarium inspiration for the opening is documented: "We wanted to give a feeling of loneliness without making it look boring." But that statement explains a mood, not a complete plot key.

The careful conclusion is narrower and more useful: GRIS tells its story through planned colour progression, statues, and level and camera readability. Where those channels lead is open to interpretation.
