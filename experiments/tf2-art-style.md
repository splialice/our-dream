## Short answer

Valve's own 2007 paper calls the approach **Illustrative Rendering in Team Fortress 2** and grounds it in "early 20th century commercial illustration with 1960s industrial design elements." The team wrote three motives into the paper: differentiation, over-the-top gameplay and class readability. It is often called cel shaded, but Valve said it did not want that route, using rim highlights and warm-to-cool hue shifts instead of dark outlines.

## What it looks like

Team Fortress 2 reads like a 1930s magazine advertisement brought into a first-person shooter: strong character silhouettes, exaggerated proportions, painted-looking shading and colours that separate classes instantly. Shadows move towards cool hues rather than black, and forms are picked out with rim highlights instead of heavy ink contours.

That is the main reason the cel-shaded label is misleading. A dark outline or a flattened shadow can look "cartoon" without coming from a stepped-lighting shader. Our [What Is Cel Shading?](/posts/what-is-cel-shading.html/) guide sets out the technical distinction; here the useful point is that Valve described a whole illustrative system, not one shader trick.

## What the developers said

The [NPAR 2007 paper](https://steamcdn-a.akamaihd.net/apps/valve/2007/NPAR07_IllustrativeRenderingInTeamFortress2.pdf) is unusually direct. Its title supplies the name used here, and its introduction locates the visual references in early 20th-century commercial illustration with 1960s industrial design. The paper names J. C. Leyendecker, Dean Cornwell and Norman Rockwell, and the deck records the shading rules: "Shading obeys a warm-to-cool hue shift. Shadows go to cool, not black", and "Silhouettes are often emphasized with rim highlights rather than dark outlines."

In a [December 2007 interview](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/valve-s-francke-game-art-direction-in-its-infancy-), art director Moby Francke said TF2 had already passed through "about four different iterations, four different art styles" when he arrived in 2002. He called the result "art by consensus": "So for Team Fortress, I had to continually say why we're doing this. Silhouettes have to be this way. Colors, through gameplay and playtesting, we were able to achieve."

A few weeks later, [GamesIndustry.biz](https://www.gamesindustry.biz/to-be-franke) recorded the same priority in plainer language: "Players need to get it straight away and we've done that with the silhouette and then the colours of each character." Francke added: "It's not just branding and a pretty picture, it's also a language by which people can communicate."

None of these sources presents a single one-word brand name for the look. They do present a consistent answer to "what is it": an illustrative rendering system built from commercial-illustration references and tuned for class readability.

## How the visual direction was developed

The 2007 paper wrote the goals down at the time: differentiation from contemporary photorealistic shooters, support for cartoonish over-the-top gameplay, and class differentiation. That last goal is where the art style becomes a design tool. The paper describes a visual read hierarchy — team, class, selected weapon — and says the nine silhouettes were tested in pure black before internal shading was added. Our [Why Game Characters Need Distinct Silhouettes](/posts/why-game-characters-need-distinct-silhouettes.html/) piece covers that Valve/Riot design problem in more detail.

The route was not straight. Francke's 2007 interview describes several failed art directions; Valve's [2008 blog post](https://www.teamfortress.com/post.php?id=1783) adds that claymation was tried and quickly abandoned. Dhabih Eng wrote that the team wanted to keep the idea of "simulating real world artistic materials in the game", which "led to the more painterly look which eventually shipped with the product."

The cel-shaded denial came in a [2008 report of Francke's Gamefest talk](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/art/in-depth-how-valve-makes-art-to-enhance-gameplay): "We didn't want to go the cel-shaded route, we wanted to go more true to the way that lighting occurs." That sentence is about Valve's own pipeline. It does not deny that players reasonably use cel shading as a visual shorthand, nor does it describe third-party mods.

## Common confusions

**Illustrative rendering versus cel shading.** The first names a broad art-and-rendering approach; the second names a stepped-lighting technique. Valve used rim highlights, warm-to-cool hue shifts and a mix of photorealistic and non-photorealistic shading. The [full change-of-direction history](/posts/why-team-fortress-2-stopped-looking-realistic.html/) has the dated sources behind that distinction.

**TF2 versus Borderlands.** Both games were called cel shaded by players and both studios resisted the label, but the pipelines were different. Gearbox chose `Concept Art Style` and described hand-drawn textures plus graphic-novel outlines; Valve described commercial-illustration references and rim highlights. See [What Is the Borderlands Art Style Called?](/posts/borderlands-art-style.html/) for the contrast.

Evidence limits: the paper is a technical description of a shipped style, not a dated project log. The interviews are contemporaneous but retrospective in parts; Francke's "about four iterations" is his estimate. No source here dates the exact meeting where the realistic direction was abandoned. The cel-shaded quotation is a media report of a conference talk, not a Valve-authored document.
