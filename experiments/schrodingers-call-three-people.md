Some visual novels feel hand-built because they were. *Schrödinger's Call* was, according to producer Makotoi Hayashi, essentially made by three people over four years. The surprising part is not just the team size. It is how they worked: not by splitting tasks into neat departments, but by building one scene together, again and again.

## Three people, one thing

AUTOMATON's June 2026 feature reports Hayashi's account:

> "According to Makotoi Hayashi, the title's producer at Shueisha Games, Schrödinger's Call was essentially made by three people over the course of four years."

One person directed. Another wrote scenarios based on the concept. Then that person made music and visuals, put them into the scene, and tested immediately. Hayashi's description is almost a production manifesto:

> "They've repeated that process an extraordinary number of times. I think that's why the game's presentation is so detailed down to every single second. Rather than dividing responsibilities, the three of them create one thing together."

That is a useful contrast with the usual idea of game production as a pipeline. Here the pipeline is a loop: write, make, insert, test, repeat. The distinction matters. They were not finishing the music, the visuals and the text in separate rooms and joining them at the end. Each element went into the scene early and rough, so the team could check whether it worked with the others in that exact moment, then change it and check again.

## The "Schrödinger's Script"

Co-director Irimajiri described the team's custom workflow as something close to programming:

> "Broadly speaking, we built it all around a kind of scripting language, something fairly close to programming."

Then came the detail that makes the process feel almost absurdly efficient:

> "We cram text, effects, and sound together into it all at once (…) it's somewhat similar to Ren'Py or TyranoScript, but we've customized it specifically for Schrödinger's Call."

Irimajiri calls it "Schrödinger's Script" and says, "We write it line by line." The point is not that the team invented a new engine from nothing. It is that they built a small internal language for the exact thing they needed: timing text, effects and sound together as one scene.

## Fifteen-minute music, ten-minute visuals

The most vivid example of the loop is Irimajiri's description of rapid prototyping:

> "I'd throw together a piece of music in about fifteen minutes and insert it, write some dialogue, quickly create rough visuals in ten minutes, and add those too… On my PC, the video software, music tools, editor, and Unity are all running simultaneously."

This is the sort of detail that makes a game's polish legible. The final presentation may look seamless, but the method was brutally practical: make a rough version fast, put it in the scene, see if it works.

## Why the collaboration story matters

![Schrödinger's Call text and art combined](/img/editorial/schrodingers-call-03.jpg)

*Official screenshot from the Schrödinger's Call Steam page. © Acrobatic Chirimenjako / SHUEISHA GAMES.*

The AUTOMATON feature brought the creators together with *The House in Fata Morgana* author Keika Hanada. Her verdict was precise: "Every aspect of it comes together like a comprehensive work of art." That reaction is useful because it points at the thing a player feels but cannot easily name: timing.

When text, music, effects and art all arrive at the right moment, the result does not feel like four systems working side by side. It feels like one hand. Hayashi's "three of them create one thing together" explains why.

This is also why the game is a good fit for the editorial side of Pixel Dango. The story is not "what genre is this?" It is "how did a tiny team get this level of control over the player's attention?" That is a production question with an answer you can trace.
