Most games use character designs to tell you who someone is. *Lost Museum: Echoes of the Chromageists* starts with a stranger question: what if a character's head were a sunflower, a water lily, or another object drawn from a painting?

That is not a random visual gag. Producer Honoka Ohara explained that the game's Chromageists are beings who live inside paintings, and the object-head design is the point.

## The design choice: no human reveal

The game describes itself as an adventure title about paintings and "Chromageists" — characters in the object-head tradition. Ohara said she already knew the trope and found it fascinating, but thought it was underused.

> "I got the impression there aren't many games that feature such characters. And among the games in which object heads do appear, they often turn out to be human, or they serve as side characters instead of being the main focus."

Then came the decision that gives the game its identity:

> "Since object heads already have such a strong visual impact, I thought, 'there's no need for them to turn back into humans.'"

That line is the whole design in miniature. In many stories, a strange figure is a disguise; the payoff is revealing the ordinary person underneath. Ohara wanted the opposite. The strange form is not a secret to solve. It is the character.

The game even stages that question directly: one scene asks the hero what he is and offers "Chromageist," "human" and "fairy" as answers. "Human" is just one option on the table, not the destination.

## Why paintings, and why Van Gogh?

The object heads are tied to the museum premise. Ohara described Chromageists as being like *tsukumogami* — objects that come alive — but dwelling in paintings:

> "Chromageists are like tsukumogami (inanimate objects that come alive and acquire a spirit) who dwell within paintings."

The idea was not just "make a game about famous art." Ohara said she wanted players who already love museums to enjoy it, but also players who recognise famous paintings without ever visiting a museum to care about the characters first, then the real artwork:

> "I hope that they first fall in love with the Chromageists, then become interested in the real-life paintings that inspired them, and that this eventually leads them to visit an art museum."

Van Gogh is the first anchor because he is recognisable and because his life supplies stories. Ohara said she did not want to make him the central focus, but chose *Sunflowers* and *The Starry Night* for a possible first installment because he has many fans in Japan and "an artist whose life is full of interesting anecdotes."

There is also a small, very human detail behind the art: Ohara said she first thought *Almond Blossom* was just a pretty painting, then learned Van Gogh made it to celebrate the birth of his brother Theo's son. After that, the painting felt happy to her. That is the kind of emotional shortcut the game is built around: a fact changes how an image feels.

## The mechanic is also a visual metaphor

The game is not only dialogue. Ohara described a loop of visual-novel scenes, point-and-click exploration, "Chromageist Appraisal," and a final "Restoration" phase. The playful hook is a one-line puzzle:

> "A unique part of the game is the 'connecting stars' action, where the player has to solve a one-line drawing puzzle in order to recover the Chromageists' memories."

A one-line drawing puzzle is a neat fit for a game about paintings. It turns looking into doing. The player does not just read that a painting has meaning; they physically trace a path through it to recover memory.

## Why it works as a story hook

The game has an easy premise to explain in one sentence: **an art museum mystery where the paintings have object-headed spirits**. That is already shareable. But the more interesting choice is the refusal to normalise the characters.

If the Chromageists became humans, the visual idea would be a gimmick with a reveal. By keeping them object-headed, the game asks the player to treat sunflowers, water lilies and other painted objects as people from the first minute. That is strange, but it also matches the museum premise: the game wants you to look at objects as carriers of stories.

Ohara said the team paid close attention to details like Sunny's belt and Nephia's mermaid skirt because they were aiming at a visual impact that "other games don't have." You can disagree with the execution, but the target is clear: make the character design do the world-building.
